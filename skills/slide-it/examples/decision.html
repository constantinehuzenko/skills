    Is one espresso machine enough?

    Not in the rush. The bar makes 60 drinks an hour; at 80 the line reaches the door and one in four walks out.

    At 80 an hour: about 6 in line (6 min), and 21 an hour walk out, 1 in 4.

    How the line behaves
    • A line forms before the bar is full: orders come in bunches, so at 50 an hour two people already wait.
    • 60 drinks an hour is one two-group machine with its barista, the low end of the 60–80 suppliers quote.
    • Past the door the line stops growing: people judge a line by its length and leave.

    What does the wait cost?

    About 15 drinks walk out every morning: $57 of margin, $1,700 a month. Patient customers cut that, but the line runs out the door.

    Where the $57 comes from
    • A drink sells for $5.00 on average; $3.80 of it is margin after coffee, milk and cup.
    • 15 walk-outs × $3.80 = $57 a morning; over 30 mornings, about $1,700 a month.

    What changes with two machines?

    The line all but disappears: at 80 an hour, walk-outs fall from 21 an hour to about one.

    Illustrative café · same customers, both cafés

    80 an hour

    Same 80 an hour. One machine: 21 an hour walk out, 1 in 4. Two: about one an hour walks out.

    Why two do more than double
    • Both machines take from one line, so one slow drink doesn't hold everyone up.
    • At 80 an hour each machine is busy two-thirds of the time; the line averages under one person.
    • Two hold until about 110 an hour; past that, the line reaches the door again.

    When does it pay back?

    In about ten months if the rush peaks at 80 an hour. At 65 it takes over two years.

    Illustrative café · $15,000 machine

    80 an hour

    At a peak of 80 an hour, the machine nets $1,570 a month: paid back in month 10.

    The payback sum
    • Each month = (drinks lost with one machine − with two) × $3.80 × 30 mornings − $90 to run it.
    • $15,000 covers the machine, a second grinder and plumbing.
    • Assumes the person on the till runs it in the rush. A new hire for two rush hours (about $1,200 a month) stretches payback at 80 to about 3½ years.

    What else could fix it?

    Try batch brew first: it saves most walk-outs for $1,800. Buy the machine when the line reaches the door again.

    Illustrative café · 80 an hour

    Fix

    A faster grinder (70 an hour) cuts walk-outs from 21 to 14 an hour: 6 of 15 drinks a morning saved.

    The fixes side by side
    • Batch brew assumes 1 in 4 rush orders is black coffee now made on the machine; poured from an urn, those skip the line.
    • A grind-on-demand grinder trims about 8 seconds a drink: 60 → 70 an hour.
    • The trigger: if the line still reaches the door after the cheap fixes, the rush has outgrown one machine.

    Full notes

    The café is illustrative

    No real café's numbers are used. The bar's pace sits inside the range suppliers quote; everything else (demand, prices, costs, the 1 in 4 black-coffee share) is invented to be plausible and kept consistent across the screens.

    Method

    Orders arrive at random (a Poisson stream) and each drink takes a random time averaging one minute (exponential). One line feeds the machines; anyone who finds 8 people waiting walks out. This is the textbook M/M/c queue with a finite line. Screens 1, 3 and 5 give its steady state for one busy hour; screens 2 and 4 solve the same model second by second through a morning whose demand rises from 20 an hour to a peak at 8:15 and falls back by 10. Waits follow Little's law (people in line = arrival rate × wait). The scenes run the same model with random customers, so their tallies wander around the stated averages.
