What do headphones cancel?

    Sound is a ripple of squeezed air that travels. The air itself only rocks in place.

    More on sound
    • Sound in air is a longitudinal wave: each bit of air rocks back and forth along the way the sound travels, and only the squeezes and gaps move on.
    • In 20 °C air, sound travels about 343 m/s at every pitch, so a higher pitch packs the squeezes closer: one wave is 343 ÷ the frequency, in metres.
    • People hear roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, waves from 17 m long down to 1.7 cm.

    How can sound cancel sound?

    Play the same wave upside down. Every push meets an equal pull, and the air at your ear stays still.

    Why timing is everything
    • Anti-noise has the same strength as the noise and an inverted phase. Adding the two is called destructive interference.
    • Late by part of a wave, the anti-noise leaves 2 × sin(180° × that part) of the noise. That breaks even at a sixth of a wave and doubles the sound at half a wave. The scene computes exactly this.

    Where does anti-noise come from?

    A microphone outside the cup hears the noise first. A chip flips it, and the speaker plays it before the noise reaches you.

    The race in numbers
    • This is the feedforward design, with the microphone outside the cup. Feedback designs listen inside the cup, at your ear; hybrid headphones use both to cancel across a wider range.
    • IEEE calls latency the fundamental constraint: the trip from microphone to speaker has to finish within a fraction of one wave of the noise.
    • Illustrative: 2 cm of air takes 58 µs at 343 m/s, and 1 cm takes 29 µs. Real cups, chips and speakers differ.
    • Being 30 µs late is 0.3% of a 100 Hz wave but 30% of a 10,000 Hz one. That is the next screen.

    Why do voices get through?

    A small timing slip barely touches a slow hum but spoils the match on quicker, higher sound. Much of a voice is quick and high.

    What else lets voices through
    • Active cancelling suits low frequencies best; IEEE puts the practical limit below roughly 1 to 2 kHz.
    • Short waves are the other limit: cancelling them would need the microphones and speaker closer to the eardrum than headphones can manage.
    • A voice's pitch is low (typically 90–155 Hz for adult men, 165–255 Hz for women), yet phone lines carry only about 300–3,400 Hz and speech stays clear. The words don't need the deep notes anti-noise handles best.
    • Voices are also short and changing. Steady drones like engines and air conditioning cancel best.
    • The model: a fixed 0.1 ms slip, and a chip that fades its anti-noise out between 800 and 2,500 Hz so it never adds noise. Both illustrative.

    What do the cups add?

    Cushions and a tight seal stop the high sounds anti-noise misses, so each covers the other's gap.

    Between you and the noise

    Passive and active together
    • Passive blocking (padding and a seal) works better the higher the pitch; active cancelling works best low. Noise-cancelling headphones combine the two.
    • A better seal blocks more outside sound, and the seal and the cancelling work best together.
    • The shares in the scene (75% of the hum and 12% of the hiss past the cushion, 10% of the hum past the anti-noise) are illustrative, not measurements.
