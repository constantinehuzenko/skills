    How does the distance build?

    Your Sunday run grows a little each week, never by more than a tenth: 5 km in week 1, 8.3 km by week 7.

    1234567Race

    The whole 8 weeks
    • Three runs a week. Monday and Friday off; Wednesday and Saturday optional easy cross-training, 30 to 45 minutes of cycling, swimming or walking. That shape is Hal Higdon's Novice 10K; the distances are this page's.
    • Each long run is at most 10% longer than the longest before it. In a 2025 study of 5,205 runners, one run more than 10% past the longest of the previous 30 days came with more overuse injuries.
    • Race day is the one planned jump, 8.3 to 10 km. Higdon's novice plan also stops short, at 5.5 miles (8.9 km). Walk breaks in the scene (1 minute every 10) are illustrative.

    Why rest days?

    Every run adds fatigue as well as fitness. Rest days drain the fatigue before the next run piles more on.

    Days off running each week plan: 4

    How the tank works
    • An illustrative model, after Banister's fitness–fatigue idea: each run adds to both; fatigue drains within days (here a 5-day time constant), fitness over weeks (40 days). The limit is a drawing, not a measured threshold.
    • The plan's three runs a week with a day off between matches NHS Couch to 5K and Higdon. Easy cross-training counts as a day off running.
    • Extra runs here are 4.5 km each. Five days off is safe too, but fitness climbs more slowly.

    What happens in race week?

    Run about half as much, but keep running. Fatigue drains in days and fitness fades over weeks, so you start fresh.

    Weeks of cutting back plan: 1

    Race week, day by day
    • Monday rest · Tuesday 4 km easy · Wednesday rest or easy cross-training · Thursday 3 km easy · Friday and Saturday rest · Sunday the race. That's 7 km against week 7's 17.3: a 60% cut, same days.
    • A meta-analysis of tapering (Bosquet 2007) found the best results from about two weeks with volume cut by 41–60%, keeping the intensity and the number of sessions. A short race after a short build needs only the last week.
    • The tanks are the same illustrative model as the rest-day screen; cutting back here halves each run. Fatigue is drawn three times heavier than fitness, so the narrow tank is a third as wide.

    Missed a week. Now what?

    Repeat the week you missed; don't skip ahead. Injuries rise when one run beats your longest of the past month by over 10%.

    Weeks missed, from week 4

    The numbers behind the 10%
    • Frandsen et al. (BJSM 2025) followed 5,205 runners and 588,000 runs for 18 months. Against runs within 10% of the longest of the past 30 days, overuse injuries came at 1.64 times the rate after a run 10–30% longer, 1.52 times after 30–100% longer and 2.28 times after more than double.
    • Weekly mileage changes showed no clear link in that study, and the old weekly 10% rule has little support: in a trial of 532 novices, a 13-week programme built on it had the same injury rate as a standard 8-week one (20.8% vs 20.3%).
    • It's observational: risk rose, which doesn't prove the spike caused it. If more than a month has passed, this page suggests restarting from a long run you've done recently, and choosing a later race over cramming.

    Sources

    • NHS, Get running with Couch to 5K: three runs a week with rest days between; week 1 alternates 1 minute of running with 90 seconds of walking; 30 minutes non-stop by the end.
    • Hal Higdon, Novice 10K training programme: 8 weeks, Monday and Friday rest, easy cross-training Wednesday and Saturday, a Sunday long run from 3 to 5.5 miles, conversational pace, walk when you need to.
    • Frandsen et al., How much running is too much? Identifying high-risk running sessions in a 5200-person cohort study, British Journal of Sports Medicine 2025;59:1203–1210 (read through the BJSM blog's summary).
    • Buist et al., No effect of a graded training program on the number of running-related injuries in novice runners, American Journal of Sports Medicine 2008;36(1):33–39.
    • Bosquet et al., Effects of tapering on performance: a meta-analysis, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise 2007;39(8):1358–1365.

    What is illustrative

    The plan's distances are this page's, set to the sources' rules. The tanks on screens 3 and 4 are a fitness–fatigue model with made-up constants (fatigue 5 days, fitness 40, the rim, the 3× weight); they show the shape of the idea, not your numbers. The coil on screen 2 isn't to scale, and its walk breaks are an example.

    Before you start

    Higdon's plan assumes no major health problems. If you have a condition, or pain that changes how you run, ask a doctor or physiotherapist first.
